## How to use this bank

The individual mark (75 %) is decided mainly in the Q&A, and the discussion group prepares its questions from our slides. Each presenter owns the section with their name and must also be able to answer the eight general questions. Every answer is 2–4 sentences, gives the exact number and the results file, and is honest about weaknesses. Three answer frameworks, tagged at the end of each answer:

- **Acknowledge · reason · leave room** — concede the premise, give the mechanism, say what would change the answer.
- **Quantify** — put the claim on the scale of the 5-fold CV standard deviation (0.007–0.011 AUC) or the paired test-set bootstrap (B = 500 in `results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv`; B = 1,000 in `results/threshold_vs_model_saving.json`).
- **Admit · give direction** — when something was not done, say so and name the next step.

Four phrases to use verbatim: "chosen on training CV, scored once on the frozen test set; the test set only reports" · "the threshold matters more than the small AUC gap" · "re-balancing moves the threshold, not the ranking" · "the clusters are a map for policy, not a better model". Two phrases never to use: "the test set was touched once" (bootstrap and cost curves do read the test rows — they select nothing) and "economically meaningful segments".

## G. General questions (all three presenters) — 8

**G1. Describe your protocol in one minute. Where exactly was the test set used?**

One stratified 70/30 split with seed 2607526, written once to `data/processed/`; every scaler, one-hot encoder, PCA, K-means and resampler is a pipeline step refitted inside each of the five training folds, every hyper-parameter is chosen by `GridSearchCV` on those folds scored by ROC-AUC, and both decision thresholds come from training out-of-fold probabilities (`code/common.py`, `tune_threshold`). Models are ordered and the reference model is chosen by training CV AUC (OOF AUC for the subsample-tuned RBF SVM) — `results/model_comparison.csv` columns `select_auc` and `rank_cv_auc`; test metrics are reported beside them and never used to select (`results/aggregate_summary.json`, `selection.rule`). The test set is read in two places: once to score each frozen configuration, and by the reporting-only scripts (paired bootstrap, ROC/PR and cost curves at the training-chosen thresholds), which select nothing — so the honest sentence is "chosen on training CV; each frozen configuration scored once on the test set; the test set only reports". *Framework: quantify.*

**G2. What does "frozen" mean, and how can we check it?**

A configuration is frozen the first time it is scored on the test set: `code/freeze.py` writes its SHA-256 and the hash of its test-probability vector into `results/frozen_manifest.json`, and `common.evaluate()` refuses to score a changed configuration under a frozen slug before any test label is read. The manifest has 46 entries: 35 from round 1 (34 models plus the leakage negative control, first scored 16–18 Sep 2026, before the professor's 30 Sep comment), 2 from RUAN's 30 Sep second pass (`knn_v2`, `logistic_plain_v2`, adopted 21 Oct), and 9 Platt-recalibrated variants scored on 19 Oct. `python3 code/freeze.py --check` re-hashes every stored `frozen/*_p_test.npy` and passes; anyone with the repository can run it. *Framework: quantify.*

**G3. Who did what? Give the contribution split.**

By model family, as in the proposal: RUAN — KNN, the logistic family, the nested-CV second pass, PCA/K-means and the stability analysis; LU — pruned tree, random forest and bagging, GBM and XGBoost, TreeSHAP, monotone XGBoost, partial dependence; LI — RBF and linear SVM, MLP, LDA/QDA/naive Bayes, the class-imbalance study, Platt recalibration, the shared `common.py` contract, `aggregate.py` and `freeze.py`. The per-section percentages in the report (DRAFT, group to confirm) are: Introduction 30/20/50, Data 25/15/60, Methods 30/25/45, Results 30/35/35, Interpretation 15/60/25, Operating point 40/15/45, Conclusion 34/33/33, Code 20/25/55, Presentation 33/33/34 (RUAN / LU / LI; `slides/contribution_statement_draft.md`). Each result JSON carries a `member` field, so the ownership is auditable file by file. *Framework: quantify.*

**G4. Did you use AI tools, and how?**

Yes, as a coding and typesetting assistant: drafting script skeletons, LaTeX, figure code and the first wording of the slide notes, always from our own results files. Every number in the report, slides and this bank is produced by a script in `code/` that writes to `results/` (`code/run_all.sh` regenerates all of them and `freeze.py --check` verifies the frozen test scorings), and each of us can explain and re-run the models we own. The modelling decisions — the KNN benchmark, the freeze, the 1:1–20:1 sweep, the Platt step, the pre-registered clustering rule — were ours and were made in response to the lectures and the professor's written comment. *(Group to confirm the exact wording of the disclosure before 26 Oct.)* *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**G5. Why a paired bootstrap rather than a DeLong test for the AUC differences?**

Because we wanted one procedure that works for AUC, average precision and the cost at a threshold, and DeLong covers only AUC; the paired row bootstrap (same 8,990 test rows resampled for both models, B = 500, seed 2607526) gives percentile intervals for all three (`results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv`, `results/threshold_vs_model_saving.json` with B = 1,000). For the headline comparison the two methods would agree: XGBoost − logistic +0.0250 [+0.0183, +0.0314] is far from zero, while XGBoost − forest +0.0020 [−0.0010, +0.0051] is a tie under any test. The limitation is that with B = 500 the percentile end-points are themselves estimates: the GBM lower bound is only +0.0001 (+0.0015 [+0.0001, +0.0030], `auc_diff_2_5` in `results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv`), so we call that one "borderline", not "significant". *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**G6. Why a 5:1 cost ratio?**

It is the UCI Statlog German-credit matrix (a missed bad customer costs 5, a false alarm 1), so it is the conventional anchor, and the data make it a lower bound rather than a truth: with a defaulter's median bill of 19,868 NT\$, a loss-given-default of 50–70 % and a false alarm costing one to three months of interest margin, the implied ratio is about 10–40 (`results/cost_anchors.json`, `implied_ratio_range` 9.6–40.5). That is why 5:1 is presented as one point of a 1:1–20:1 sweep (`results/threshold_vs_model_saving.csv`, `results/cost_sensitivity.csv`): the conclusion "moving the cut-off saves more than changing the model" holds at every ratio above 1:1, and the model saving's interval includes zero at 5:1 and 20:1. The closed form t* = 1/(1 + r) gives 0.167 at 5:1 against the searched 0.15 on a flat out-of-fold cost curve. The sweep stops at 20:1, short of the data's own 40 upper anchor, because the rule is already "flag everyone" there: the logistic cut-off is 0.01 and 99.9 % of clients are flagged (`share_flagged_logistic` 0.9991 at ratio 20 in `results/threshold_vs_model_saving.csv`), so higher ratios add nothing but the trivial rule. *Framework: quantify.*

**G7. Everything rests on one 70/30 split. Why no repeated splits?**

The bootstrap quantifies test-sampling noise (XGBoost's own interval is [0.7760, 0.7999], about ±0.012) but not the variation another split would add; the fold-to-fold CV standard deviation (0.007–0.011) is only a proxy. Two things make us comfortable with the ranking: nested CV reproduces the plain 5-fold figures for KNN (0.7684 ± 0.0073 vs 0.7700) and the logistic family (0.7602 vs 0.7602) within one standard error (`results/nested_cv_summary_repro.json`), and the test AUC of every headline model sits within one CV standard deviation of its CV AUC (`results/model_comparison.csv`). Repeating the whole pipeline over five to ten seeds is the direct fix; it was outside the compute budget and, after the freeze, would have required a fresh hold-out to avoid turning the test set into a validation set. *Framework: admit · give direction.*

**G8. What did the professor's 30 Sep comment change in your project?**

Four things, each visible in the deliverables. (1) Freeze: nothing was re-scored after 18 Sep except two already-frozen configurations in RUAN's second pass and the nine Platt variants, all recorded in `results/frozen_manifest.json`; nested CV confirmed the frozen KNN and logistic choices without consulting the test set (`results/nested_cv_summary_repro.json`). (2) Threshold over AUC and 5:1 as a range: every "N times" claim became two absolute savings with intervals over nine ratios (`results/threshold_vs_model_saving.csv`). (3) Clustering secondary: we adopted PCA + K = 5, measured bootstrap stability and the incremental out-of-sample AUC/AP of the cluster label with a Nadeau–Bengio interval, found +0.0001 [−0.0004, +0.0007], and withdrew the test-set evaluation of the cluster-augmented logit (`results/unsupervised_summary_repro.json`). (4) Resampling inside folds and recalibration: the nine weighted/resampled models were Platt-recalibrated on training OOF scores (`results/recalibration.json`). *Framework: quantify.*

## R. RUAN Hangjing — slides 1–5 (title, problem/data, EDA, protocol, benchmark + linear family) — 14

**R1. Why this dataset, and is a 2005 Taiwanese sample still a reasonable basis?**

It is the UCI *Default of Credit Card Clients* set (Yeh & Lien 2009): 30,000 card holders of one Taiwanese bank observed April–September 2005, labelled by whether the October payment was missed; 22.1 % defaulters, zero missing cells, 35 exact duplicates dropped to 29,965 rows (`results/eda_summary.json`, `results/eda_data_quality.csv`). A public set lets the marker check every number, and 8,990 test rows give bootstrap intervals of about ±0.012 AUC. What is dated is the level of the variables, not the mechanism: recency and severity of arrears, utilisation and the payment-to-bill ratio drive default in any card book, while the NT\$ cut-offs and the 22 % base rate belong to that bank and year. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**R2. What does the EDA say drives default, and was it done on the training rows only?**

Every group rate, correlation and univariate AUC uses the 20,975 training rows; only the data-quality checks and the target count use all 29,965 (`results/eda_summary.json`, `analysis_partition`). Arrears dominate: the training default rate is 12.9 % at PAY_1 = 0, 33.9 % one month late and 67.6 % two months late; N_MONTHS_DELAYED alone has univariate AUC 0.7235 (0 months 11.8 %, 6 months 69.3 %); the limit gradient runs 32.1 % in the lowest quintile to 13.7 % in the highest; utilisation is U-shaped — 24.2 % in the lowest decile, a 14.4 % trough at 0.04–0.13, 28.1 % at the top. The six bills correlate at 0.80–0.95 (19 feature pairs above 0.8), which matters for the linear models' coefficients, not for prediction. *Framework: quantify.*

**R3. Why K = 101 for KNN? That is far from the K = 5 used in class.**

The grid was K ∈ {5, 7, …, 101, 151} × {uniform, distance}, 24 settings on five stratified folds scored by AUC; CV AUC rises from 0.7061 at K = 5 to 0.7700 ± 0.0087 at K = 101 and flattens (0.7688 at K = 151), so the optimum is interior (`results/knn.json`, `results/knn_k5.json`). Default is a noisy 22 % outcome with overlapping classes, so the local default proportion needs about a hundred neighbours to be a usable probability: K = 5 has train AUC 0.883 against test 0.709 — a memorising vote — while K = 101 has train 0.787, OOF 0.770 and test 0.771. Nested CV with the inner grid re-run in each outer fold chooses K = 101 in three folds and 151 in two, and gives 0.7684 ± 0.0073 (`results/nested_cv_knn_repro.json`), so the message is "a large K", not the number 101. *Framework: quantify.*

**R4. Why is KNN the benchmark, and does it really beat logistic regression?**

It is the course's non-parametric yardstick: any advanced model that cannot beat its CV 0.7700 / test 0.7706 must be explained, and the RBF SVM (0.7606 / 0.7616), logistic (0.7602 / 0.7636), LDA (0.7572 / 0.7592) and naive Bayes (0.7356 / 0.7398) do not (`results/model_comparison.csv`). The gap to the logit is 0.0098 on CV, about one fold standard deviation, and we did not bootstrap that pair directly, so the honest wording is "comparable, KNN marginally ahead", probably because KNN follows the jump between paid-duly and one-to-two months late that an additive logit can only approximate. The operational objection stands: KNN stores the whole 20,975 × 32 matrix (5.4 MB) and scores 8,990 clients in 0.379 s against 0.0055 s for the logit, with no coefficients (`results/knn.json`, `extra.reference_logistic`). *Framework: quantify.*

**R5. What did your nested-CV second pass show, and why did it not change anything?**

Hyper-parameters were re-chosen inside each of the five outer folds, so the outer AUC carries no selection optimism: KNN 0.7684 ± 0.0073 against the plain 5-fold 0.7700, and the logistic family 0.7602 / 0.7601 / 0.7602 / 0.7608 / 0.7608 for plain / ridge / lasso / weighted / weighted + Platt with standard errors of about 0.005 (`results/nested_cv_summary_repro.json`, `results/logistic_nested_cv_v2.csv`). The rules were fixed in advance: the one-standard-error rule keeps the plain logit, and class weighting is adopted only if its cross-fitted 5:1 cost falls by more than 1 % — it fell by 0.43 %, so it was not adopted. Both selections agree with the configurations frozen on 16 Sep (`agrees_with_frozen: true`), so the first-round tuning did not flatter the CV figures and nothing had to be re-scored on the test set. *Framework: quantify.*

**R6. Your 30 Sep proposal said the logistic cost was 7,065 at 0.5 and 5,141 at a 0.17 cut-off; the report says 7,063 and 5,126 at 0.15. Which is right?**

Both are the same plain logit refitted twice: my 30 Sep run and the 16 Sep frozen scoring differ by solver tolerance only (test AUC 0.763679 vs 0.763634; at 0.5, 310 vs 308 false positives, cost 7,065 vs 7,063; `results/logistic_plain_v2.json`, `results/logistic.json`). The out-of-fold 5:1 cost curve is flat between 0.15 and 0.17, so each run picked a different point on the plateau; on the test rows the two cut-offs cost 5,141 and 5,126, a difference of 0.3 % (same two files; the nine-ratio view of my refit is `results/logistic_cost_sensitivity_v2.csv`). The report uses one coherent set, the 16 Sep scoring of every model, and footnotes the proposal-stage figures (threshold saving 1,924 [1,634, 2,193], model saving 160 [−8, 331] at 5:1 in `results/threshold_vs_model_saving_v2.csv`, against 1,937 [1,604, 2,218] and 145 [−18, 299] in the frozen table). *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**R7. Ridge and lasso give the same AUC as the plain logit. Why does regularisation buy nothing?**

CV AUC is 0.7602 for all three and test AUC 0.7636; the tuned L2 C = 31.6 shrinks almost nothing and the L1 optimum at C = 100 keeps all 32 columns (`results/logistic_family_summary.csv`). With n/p = 20,975/32 ≈ 655 the maximum-likelihood variance is already tiny, so there is no variance to trade for bias; the 0.76 ceiling is the bias of linearity, not estimation noise. Nested CV says the same — plain, ridge and lasso tie within one standard error (0.7602 / 0.7601 / 0.7602, `results/logistic_nested_cv_v2.csv`) — which is why the pre-registered 1-SE rule keeps the plain model, whose p-values are exact. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**R8. PAY_2–PAY_6 have negative coefficients although late payment raises default, and your largest coefficient is EDUCATION = others. Paradoxes?**

Neither. Given the same number and severity of delays — absorbed by N_MONTHS_DELAYED (+0.634 per SD, odds ratio 1.88) and MAX_DELAY (+0.366) — a delay in an older month is less dangerous than the same delay now, so the raw statuses encode recency; the same logic puts UTIL_1 at −0.259 next to UTIL_MEAN at +0.288 (`results/logistic_coefficients.csv`). EDUCATION = others (−1.070, odds ratio 0.34, p = 2 × 10⁻⁶) is the recoded undocumented codes 0/5/6 — 343 training rows at a 6.7 % default rate (`results/eda_summary.json`) — a quirk of this dataset, not an education effect; the forest ranks EDUCATION 27th of 29 by MDI and 24th by permutation (18th by out-of-bag permutation; `results/rf_importance.csv`). RUAN's 30 Sep refit reproduces every sign and the ordering of the coefficients quoted here, each within 0.02 of the 16 Sep value (`results/logistic_odds_ratios_v2.csv` vs `results/logistic_coefficients.csv`: EDUCATION_4 −1.061 vs −1.070, N_MONTHS_DELAYED 0.633 vs 0.634, MAX_DELAY 0.367 vs 0.366, UTIL_MEAN 0.274 vs 0.288, UTIL_1 −0.242 vs −0.259). *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**R9. What did class weighting do to the logistic model, and why did you not adopt it?**

Almost exactly an intercept shift: `class_weight='balanced'` moves the intercept from −1.305 to −0.063 and changes no slope by more than 0.075 (`results/logistic_balanced.json`, `extra`); a monotone shift cannot change the ranking, so test AUC is 0.7630 against 0.7636 and the price is calibration, Brier 0.138 → 0.190. Platt scaling repairs the scale (Brier 0.1389, cut-off back to 0.15; `results/recalibration.json`) but the nested-CV rule asked for a cost gain above 1 % and the cross-fitted 5:1 cost moved by −0.43 % (`results/nested_cv_summary_repro.json`), so the plain model plus an explicit business threshold is the recommendation. The balanced model at 0.5 agrees with the plain model at 0.22 on 99.4 % of test clients (`results/imbalance_threshold_equivalence.csv`). *Framework: quantify.*

**R10. Why 10 principal components, and why does a logistic regression on PCs do worse?**

Ten PCs of the 32 preprocessed columns carry 81.8 % of the variance (two PCs 48.1 %, fifteen 92.2 %; `results/unsupervised_summary_repro.json`, `pca_cum_var`), enough to make K-means distances meaningful without keeping noise components. As an input to a logit they hurt: in the 5 × 5 repeated CV on training rows a logistic on the 10 PCs loses 0.034 AUC against the full logit (−0.034 [−0.040, −0.029]) and 0.016 AP, because the predictive signal — the recency contrast of arrears — sits partly in low-variance directions that PCA discards. An unpenalised logit is rotation-invariant, so using all components equals using the raw features and dropping any can only remove signal; with n/p ≈ 655 there is no variance to trade. PCA is a map for the clustering, not a model input. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**R11. Why K = 5 clusters with a silhouette of only 0.27, and how stable are they?**

K = 5 has the highest silhouette on the grid 2–8: 0.259 / 0.267 / 0.257 / **0.269** / 0.209 / 0.215 / 0.218 (`results/kmeans_selection_repro.csv`); 0.27 is weak-to-moderate and says the segments are adjacent regions of one continuous cloud, which we state. Stability over 100 bootstrap refits is high: ARI 0.957 [0.745, 0.990], per-cluster Jaccard 0.972 / 0.974 / 0.791 / 0.970 / 0.953 — the small affluent cluster (886 clients, 4.2 %) is the least reproducible — while Ward linkage agrees only moderately, ARI 0.45–0.59, mean 0.523 (`results/unsupervised_summary_repro.json`). Across random seeds the ARI falls to a mean of 0.814 and a minimum of 0.577, so the partition depends on initialisation more than the bootstrap alone suggests; that is the honest caveat. *Framework: quantify.*

**R12. Do the clusters add anything to prediction?**

No, and we tested it the way the professor asked: the one-hot cluster label added to the logit changes the out-of-sample AUC by +0.0001 [−0.0004, +0.0007] and the AP by −0.0003 [−0.0016, +0.0010] in 5 × 5 repeated CV on the training set with a Nadeau–Bengio interval (`results/unsupervised_summary_repro.json`, `results/cluster_incremental_folds_repro.csv`). The adoption rule fixed in advance required both lower bounds above zero, so the label is not used and the test set was not consulted for it. The round-1 cluster-augmented logit (`logistic_plus_clusters`) stays in the appendix as "round-1 scoring, not adopted"; we do not quote its test AUC. The label as a score on its own reaches AUC 0.648 against 0.724 for months-in-arrears alone — it is a coarse function of features the model already has. *Framework: quantify.*

**R13. Your clusters have default rates from 6.9 % to 58.5 %. Isn't that economically meaningful?**

The rates differ (16.8 / 58.5 / 6.9 / 17.9 / 18.2 %; `results/kmeans_profiles_repro.csv`) because the inputs contain the arrears and limit variables: the 58.5 % cluster averages 4.3 months delayed against 0.15–0.53 elsewhere, so the clustering has rediscovered the arrears gradient, not found a new one. A segmentation is economically distinct only if it carries information the score does not, and R12 shows it does not. What the five profiles do give is a map for policy — transactors (38 %, utilisation 4 %), mass-market revolvers (36 %, utilisation 57 %, median-sized limits), affluent heavy revolvers (9 %, limit 286k, utilisation 75 %), a small affluent low-risk group (4 %) and the delinquent 12.5 % — useful for collections priority and limit policy, descriptive only. We say "a map for policy, not a better model". *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**R14. Your proposal used K = 4 clusters on raw features. Why did the configuration change?**

The proposal's local run clustered 26 standardised raw features with the elbow rule and found K = 4; the professor asked for stability and incremental value, so the 30 Sep configuration moved to PCA on the 32 preprocessed columns (10 PCs), chose K by silhouette and added bootstrap ARI, per-cluster Jaccard, Ward agreement and the repeated-CV incremental test (`results/unsupervised_summary_v2.json`, reproduced 21 Oct as `*_repro`). The conclusions did not change — a delinquent cluster at about 60 % default appears at every K ≥ 4 in both runs, and the cluster label adds nothing to prediction in either — so the K = 4 run survives as a one-sentence robustness note. The figures are the K = 5 ones. *Framework: admit · give direction.*

## L. LU Xinyu — slides 6–7 and 13–14 (trees/forest/boosting, drivers + beyond syllabus, segments + recommendation, limitations/next) — 15

**L1. Your unpruned tree has AUC 0.61 and accuracy below the majority class. How can a model that fits the training data perfectly be that bad?**

The tree has 2,791 leaves at depth 55 and train AUC 1.000 — it memorised 20,975 noisy labels — so test AUC is 0.6125, AP 0.290 barely above the 0.221 prevalence, and accuracy 0.728 below the 0.779 majority rate (`results/tree_full.json`, `results/model_comparison.csv`). Every leaf is pure, so it emits only 0 or 1 and the ROC curve has a single operating point. That is variance in the bias–variance sense: each tiny leaf is fitted to a handful of clients whose outcome is largely noise. Pruning to 17 leaves lifts CV AUC from 0.6154 to 0.7689 and test AUC to 0.7755, and the instability of one tree is the motivation for bagging and boosting. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**L2. How did you choose the pruning parameter, and how sensitive is the result?**

Cost-complexity pruning gives 1,283 candidate alphas; 21 of them (log-spaced, 2,791 → 2 leaves) crossed with max_depth ∈ {None, 4, 6, 8} make 84 settings on five folds (`results/tree_cv_grid.csv`). The winner, alpha = 6.63 × 10⁻⁴ with depth cap 6, gives 17 leaves at depth 5 and CV AUC 0.7689 ± 0.0076; the cap is non-binding (the tree stops at depth 5). The plateau is broad — neighbouring alphas score 0.767–0.769 and a depth-4 cap alone reaches 0.763 — so only the top structure should be quoted: MAX_DELAY carries 59.8 % of the impurity reduction and PAY_1 17.3 % (`results/tree_pruned.json`, `feature_importances_top`). On CV the pruned tree is 6th of 11, tying the KNN benchmark (0.7689 vs 0.7700); on test it is ahead (0.7755 vs 0.7706) — we say "ties the benchmark on CV". *Framework: quantify.*

**L3. What does the pruned tree actually say, and why does it split on a near-zero May bill?**

The root asks whether the client was ever two or more months late (MAX_DELAY ≤ 1.5): 15,095 clients never were and default at 12.8 %, the other 5,880 at 46.1 % (`results/tree_rules.txt`, `results/tree_pruned.json` `first_splits`). On the safe side the counter-intuitive split BILL_AMT5 ≤ 789.5 isolates 3,660 low-activity accounts that default more (21.0 % vs 10.2 % elsewhere), and within them UTIL_MEAN ≤ 0 — 686 dormant or credit-balance accounts — default at 35.7 %: no billing history means no repayment signal, a known risk pocket in this dataset (`results/eda_data_quality.csv`: the non-positive-bill group defaults at 37.0 % against 21.7 % in training). The tree emits only 17 distinct probabilities, so it ranks groups, not individuals. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**L4. Why min_samples_leaf = 20 in a random forest? Averaging is supposed to remove the variance of fully grown trees.**

Averaging removes variance but not the label noise each fully grown tree memorises: in the 3 × 3 grid CV AUC is 0.7832–0.7843 at leaf size 20, 0.7744–0.7796 at 5, and only 0.7635–0.7666 at 1, where every tree has train AUC 1.000 (`results/random_forest.json`, `extra.grid`). With sklearn's default leaf of 1 the forest would barely beat the logit (0.7666 vs 0.7602). Within each leaf size the smallest feature draw (sqrt = 5 of 32 columns) wins; the winner re-scored with 500 trees goes 0.7843 → 0.7849 and is ahead of the runner-up in all five folds, and the out-of-bag AUC 0.7849 equals the CV figure, the free check we used. Both chosen values sit at a grid corner, so leaf 50 or 100 were not tried — the honest gap. *Framework: admit · give direction.*

**L5. Random forest versus bagging: is a 0.004 AUC difference worth mentioning?**

The forest (max_features = sqrt) has CV 0.7849, test 0.7866, AP 0.5756; bagging with the same leaf size and all 32 columns has CV 0.7813, test 0.7833, AP 0.5712 (`results/bagging.json`, `results/random_forest.json`). The gap is inside one fold standard deviation, but it is consistent — the out-of-bag curve shows the forest ahead at every tree count (0.7849 vs 0.7816 at 500) — and bagging overfits more (train AUC 0.912 vs 0.888) and fits in 89.4 s against 19.7 s (`fit_seconds`). The mechanism is the lecture's variance formula ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B: bagged trees all split first on the arrears variables and are highly correlated, so adding trees stops helping; drawing five random columns per split lowers ρ. We did not bootstrap this pair on the test set, so the wording is "consistently, modestly better, and cheaper". *Framework: quantify.*

**L6. XGBoost, gradient boosting and the random forest are within 0.002 of each other. Are they distinguishable?**

No. CV AUCs are 0.7863 / 0.7850 / 0.7849 and test AUCs 0.7887 / 0.7872 / 0.7866, all inside one fold standard deviation (0.007); the paired bootstrap calls the forest a tie (+0.0020 [−0.0010, +0.0051]) and the GBM borderline (+0.0015 [+0.0001, +0.0030], AP interval including zero) (`results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv`). XGBoost is the reference because it is first on training CV, not because of the test numbers. Given the tie the choice is operational: XGBoost refits in 0.5 s against 16.3 s for the GBM and 19.7 s for the forest, and 300 depth-3 trees are far smaller than 500 uncapped trees (`results/model_comparison.csv`, `fit_seconds`). *Framework: quantify.*

**L7. Why is your most flexible boosting setting the worst, and why do all ensembles stop near 0.79?**

In the 24-setting grid the worst is depth 6, learning rate 0.1, 600 rounds — CV AUC 0.7547 with train AUC 0.998 — and the best is depth 3, rate 0.03, 300 rounds with train AUC only 0.808; the learning curve at rate 0.03 peaks at 327 rounds (0.7864) and falls to 0.7794 by 1,500 rounds while training AUC keeps climbing to 0.867 (`results/xgboost.json`, `extra.grid`, `extra.learning_curve`; `results/xgb_learning_curve.csv`). Shrinkage and shallow trees are the regulariser this noisy target rewards. Four independent families reach the same ceiling — boosting 0.789, forest 0.787, MLP 0.773, KNN 0.771 on test — and pushing capacity in any of them lowers CV AUC, which looks like the irreducible noise of predicting next month's default from six months of statements without income or bureau data; we cannot separate "irreducible" from "missing features" with this dataset. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**L8. Class-weighted XGBoost has the same AUC but a much worse Brier score. Why, and what did Platt scaling do?**

With scale_pos_weight = 3.52 the test AUC is 0.7887 in both versions and AP 0.5788 vs 0.5784, because re-weighting rescales the positive-class gradient and shifts scores almost monotonically; what changes is the scale — Brier 0.1311 → 0.1772, log-loss 0.420 → 0.536, recall at 0.5 from 0.363 to 0.649 with precision 0.702 → 0.461 (`results/xgboost_weighted.json`, `results/xgboost.json`). LI's Platt step, fitted on the weighted model's training OOF scores, returns Brier to 0.1311 and the 5:1 cut-off from 0.37 to 0.14, with AUC unchanged at 0.7887 (`results/recalibration.json`, `xgboost_weighted_platt`). So weighting buys nothing that the unweighted model at its own threshold does not already give, and it breaks the probabilities until recalibrated. *Framework: quantify.*

**L9. What drives the score? Your MDI, permutation and SHAP rankings disagree.**

They agree at the top: PAY_1 is first under all three (mean |SHAP| 0.298 on the log-odds scale, MDI 0.188, permutation AUC drop 0.042), followed by MAX_DELAY (0.281) and N_MONTHS_DELAYED (0.230) under SHAP and MDI (`results/shap_importance.csv`, `results/rf_importance.csv`). They disagree in the middle — Spearman 0.43 between SHAP and MDI, 0.76 between SHAP and permutation — because MDI credits every split, so the correlated arrears columns collect Gini between them, while permuting one costs little when its siblings substitute; the amount columns are the mirror image. The drop-column refits make the point sharpest: removing PAY_1 costs 0.0058 out-of-bag AUC, removing any other single feature at most 0.0010 (`results/rf_refit_drop_column.csv`). Demographics (SEX, EDUCATION, MARRIAGE) sit in the bottom third under MDI (ranks 28 / 27 / 29) and training permutation (25 / 24 / 26) and never enter the top ten under SHAP (15 / 16 / 12) or out-of-bag permutation (11 / 18 / 14) (`results/rf_importance.csv`, `results/shap_importance.csv`). *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**L10. What is TreeSHAP, and why did you run it on the training rows?**

A feature's Shapley value is its average marginal contribution to the prediction over all orders in which features could be added, so the contributions sum exactly to the difference between this client's log-odds and the base value — TreeSHAP computes it exactly for tree ensembles, and we verified local accuracy to 3 × 10⁻⁶ on all 20,975 training rows (`results/shap_summary.json`). It is a property of the fitted model, not of a data set, so explaining the training rows costs nothing and leaves the test set unread. The worked example: training client 10,000 has predicted probability 0.861; from the base value −1.263 (probability 0.220), PAY_1 = 2 contributes +0.86 log-odds, N_MONTHS_DELAYED = 6 +0.56, MAX_DELAY = 7 +0.55 and LIMIT_BAL = 10,000 +0.22. Limits: contributions are additive on the log-odds scale only, and credit between the six bills (r = 0.80–0.95) depends on the trees actually grown. *Framework: quantify.*

**L11. What did the monotone-constrained XGBoost cost, and why build it?**

A lender must be able to say that more arrears never lowers the score; the unconstrained model violates that on the sweep — on a median client three of the ten PAY_1 steps move the probability the wrong way (PAY_1 from −1 to 0 lowers it from 0.067 to 0.065; largest fall 0.0034), and across 200 random profiles 576 of 2,000 steps do (`results/xgboost_monotone.json`, `violations`; `results/monotone_check.csv`). Constraining 13 features (11 increasing, LIMIT_BAL and PAY_RATIO_MEAN decreasing) removes every violation at a measured price: CV AUC −0.0019, test AUC −0.0016 [−0.0030, −0.0002] by paired bootstrap, AP −0.0058, cost at the tuned threshold 4,990 vs 4,981. The constrained model was configured a priori and scored once; it is the version we would deploy because the 0.002 AUC is cheaper than an unexplainable reversal in a credit decision. *Framework: quantify.*

**L12. What does partial dependence add, and what did it show about utilisation?**

PD averages the model's own prediction over all 20,975 training clients while one feature is swept, so it reads the shape the ensemble learned without touching a label (`results/pdp_summary.json`). Arrears are step-like: PAY_1 moves the probability from 0.21 at one month late to 0.372 at two and is flat beyond; N_MONTHS_DELAYED is monotone 0.190 → 0.321 with 97.6 % of individual ICE curves monotone. Utilisation is U-shaped — 0.228 at zero, a minimum of 0.204 at 0.084, 0.244 at the top — and the limit effect is 0.257 at 10,000 falling to 0.201 at 360,000. This is why the logit's additive log-odds plateau at 0.76: it cannot bend the U in utilisation or the step in arrears, and the forest and boosting can. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**L13. What is your recommendation to the bank, in one sentence each for model, threshold and segments?**

Model: deploy the XGBoost (depth 3, 300 rounds, learning rate 0.03) or, for an explainable policy, its monotone twin at 0.002 AUC less; both are calibrated (mean predicted probability 0.220 against a prevalence 0.221; `results/aggregate_summary.json`, `calibration`). Threshold: at a 5:1 cost ratio score at 0.15, not 0.5 — that alone takes the logistic cost from 7,063 to 5,126, a saving of 1,937 [1,604, 2,218], while changing to XGBoost at its own cut-off saves a further 145 [−18, 299]; re-run the sweep when the bank states its own ratio (`results/threshold_vs_model_saving.csv`). Segments: use the five K-means profiles to organise collections and limit policy — the delinquent 12.5 % hold most of the risk — but not as a model input, since they add +0.0001 AUC (`results/unsupervised_summary_repro.json`). *Framework: quantify.*

**L14. What are the limitations, and what would you do next?**

One split and one seed — the bootstrap measures test-sampling noise, not split-to-split variation (G7); the features describe the month before the label, so this is next-month monitoring of existing clients, not origination scoring; the 5:1 ratio is an anchor, and the data imply 10–40 (`results/cost_anchors.json`); and the 0.79 ceiling cannot be attributed between irreducible noise and missing income or bureau data. Next: repeated splits on a fresh hold-out, a log1p or quantile transform for the distance and Gaussian models (naive Bayes gains 0.017 test AUC with it; `results/model_comparison.csv`, `naive_bayes_log`), segment-wise calibration monitoring, and a cost matrix elicited from the lender rather than assumed. *Framework: admit · give direction.*

**L15. Your slide says bootstrap ARI 0.957 at K = 5, but the plotted K-sweep shows about 0.94. Which is it?**

Both are correct and both are on the slide: the sweep in the figure re-runs a smaller number of bootstrap refits at every K from 2 to 8 and reads 0.941 (least-stable cluster Jaccard 0.768) at K = 5 (`results/kmeans_selection_repro.csv`, row K = 5), while the dedicated K = 5 run with 100 refits gives 0.957 [0.745, 0.990] and a least-stable Jaccard of 0.791 (`results/unsupervised_summary_repro.json`). The two draws differ by 0.016 in mean ARI, inside the 100-refit interval, and the ordering of K is unchanged — K = 6 is the unstable one (0.79, Jaccard 0.67). Across random initialisations the ARI falls to a mean of 0.814, which is the honest caveat we state. *Framework: quantify.*

## J. LI Jingwen — slides 8–12 (SVM/MLP/discriminant, imbalance + recalibration, leakage, all-model ranking, operating point) — 15

**J1. Why is the RBF-kernel SVM below the KNN benchmark and no better than the logit?**

Its fair training number is the out-of-fold AUC 0.7606 on the full training set (the subsample CV figure 0.7681 is not comparable), against KNN 0.7700 and logistic 0.7602; on test 0.7616 vs 0.7636 is a tie, but AP 0.4944 vs 0.5234 and Brier 0.1448 vs 0.1383 are not, and against XGBoost the SVM's AP gap of 0.083 [0.066, 0.100] is the largest of any headline model (`results/svm_rbf.json`, `results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv`). The mechanism is the loss: hinge loss cares only about the sign of the margin, and on this overlapping problem 13,140 training rows (62.6 %) are support vectors, so the decision values are poorly spread; Platt scaling (a = −0.974, b = 1.109) is monotone and cannot repair the ordering. Two of my choices also hurt: tuning on a 6,000-row subsample and no log transform of the heavy-tailed amounts, to which an RBF distance is most sensitive. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**J2. The linear-kernel SVM scores far below every other linear model. Why?**

At the tuned C = 0.01 the exact hinge optimum is a single-feature ranker: the weight sits almost entirely on MAX_DELAY and the out-of-fold AUC is 0.7167, essentially MAX_DELAY alone; whatever the test AUC 0.7506 shows above that comes from solver residue and is not a property of the model (`results/svm_linear.json`, `notes`). Replacing hinge by squared hinge in `LinearSVC` at the same C spreads the weights over the features the logit uses and recovers logistic-level ranking: CV 0.7611, test 0.7627, AP 0.5150 (`results/svm_linear_calibrated.json`). The lesson for the slide: a linear SVM and a logistic regression share the hypothesis class w·x + b and differ only in the loss, and on a noisy 22 % target the hinge loss is the wrong one. *Framework: quantify.*

**J3. Class weighting changed nothing for the logit or XGBoost yet was decisive for your SVMs. Why?**

Logistic regression and XGBoost estimate posteriors, so re-weighting is a prior shift — for the logit an intercept move from −1.305 to −0.063 — that leaves the ranking untouched (`results/logistic_balanced.json`). The hinge loss does not estimate posteriors: with a 22 % minority the unweighted margin is placed where the majority dictates, most defaulters become bounded support vectors on the wrong side, and the decision function stops discriminating among them. Balanced weights lift the best subsample CV AUC of the RBF kernel from 0.7311 (no weighting) to 0.7681 (`results/svm_rbf.json`, `extra.best_cv_auc_by_class_weight`). Those are 6,000-row figures with fold SDs of 0.005–0.024, so the size is approximate; the direction is not. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**J4. Why does your over-parameterised MLP collapse, and what does the tuned one add?**

The 32-256-256-1 network has 74,497 weights for 20,975 rows (3.6 per row), no early stopping, and reaches train AUC 0.997 with CV 0.692 and test 0.678 — worse than naive Bayes. On an internal 85/15 split of the training set the over-parameterised net's hold-out AUC is best at epoch 1 (0.760) and falls to 0.700 by epoch 200 while training AUC reaches 0.994; even the tuned 32-64-32-1 architecture run without early stopping peaks at epoch 14 (0.762) and falls to 0.712 (train 0.891) — `results/mlp_overfit_epochs.csv` (column `network`), `results/mlp_overfit.json` and `results/mlp.json` `extra.epoch_diagnostic_train_internal_split`. The tuned 32-64-32-1 network (4,225 weights, L2 alpha 0.001, early stopping at epoch 23 with best validation epoch 7) gives CV 0.7762 ± 0.0069 and test 0.7731, 4th of 11 on CV and the best non-tree model; the 24-setting grid spans only 0.0047 AUC, so architecture barely matters once capacity is controlled. Against the logit the paired bootstrap in `results/mlp.json` (`extra.paired_bootstrap_test`, B = 2,000) gives +0.0095 [+0.0040, +0.0153]; against XGBoost −0.0155 [−0.0204, −0.0108] — the aggregate B = 500 run in `results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv` gives the same gap from XGBoost's side, mean +0.0156 [+0.0103, +0.0199], same verdict. *Framework: quantify.*

**J5. LDA, QDA and naive Bayes — why are the generative models at the bottom?**

LDA is the generative twin of the logit — same linear log-odds, parameters from class-conditional Gaussians with a pooled covariance — and lands just below it: CV 0.7572 vs 0.7602, test 0.7592, with coefficient vectors at cosine 0.934 and test scores at Spearman 0.990 to the logit (`results/lda.json`, `extra.logistic_reference`); the shared covariance is estimated on amounts with skewness 10–33, which costs the little it loses. QDA with shrinkage 0.5 reaches 0.7511 / 0.7550 because one full 32 × 32 covariance per class on skewed, dummy-laden columns is badly specified (`results/qda.json`). Gaussian naive Bayes is last (CV 0.7356, test 0.7398, Brier 0.203, worse than a constant) because the six bills correlate at a mean 0.887 and 35.8 % of its test probabilities are extreme; a signed log1p on the 13 amount columns lifts it to 0.7563 on test (`results/naive_bayes.json`, `results/model_comparison.csv`). *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**J6. Does re-balancing help? Which strategy won?**

None of them changes the ranking: for the C = 1 logit the CV AUC across none / balanced / SMOTE-in-folds / under-sampling spans 0.7597–0.7609, a range of 0.0012, and for the 500-tree forest the same four give 0.7800 / 0.7805 / 0.7745 / 0.7790 — SMOTE loses 0.008 on test (0.782 → 0.774) because synthetic minority rows blur the arrears steps (`results/imbalance_comparison.csv`). What re-balancing does change is the probability scale: mean predicted probability 0.44 instead of 0.22 and Brier 0.190 instead of 0.138 for the logit. So the strategy adopted is "none, plus a threshold chosen on training OOF for the business cost"; and every sampler ran inside the folds, as the professor asked. On CV the balanced logit is +0.0007 above the plain one — within noise — so we say "never upwards by more than noise", not "always downwards". *Framework: quantify.*

**J7. If weighting only shifts the threshold, show me.**

The balanced logit at 0.5 and the plain logit at 0.22 flag almost the same clients: agreement 99.4 % on the test set (2,794 vs 2,788 clients flagged), with the equivalent threshold 0.224 predicted from the intercept shift log(0.779/0.221) alone (`results/imbalance_threshold_equivalence.csv`). SMOTE and under-sampling give the same 0.22 equivalence at 98.9–99.0 % agreement; for the forest the equivalent plain threshold is 0.29 (balanced) or 0.34 (SMOTE) at 95–98 % agreement, lower because trees are not a pure prior shift. The slide figure `s_imbalance.png` plots exactly this. *Framework: quantify.*

**J8. What did the Platt recalibration do, and how did you keep it leakage-free?**

Platt scaling is a one-variable logistic regression of the label on logit(p), fitted on each model's training out-of-fold probabilities and nothing else; thresholds are re-chosen on cross-fitted calibrated scores, and the map is applied to the frozen test probabilities and scored once, as a new configuration registered in the manifest on 19 Oct (`results/recalibration.json`, `method`). For the nine weighted / resampled models the Brier returns to the unweighted twin — weighted XGBoost 0.1772 → 0.1311, balanced logit 0.1901 → 0.1389, balanced forest 0.1606 → 0.1335, weighted MLP 0.1855 → 0.1377 — and the 5:1 cut-off returns to 0.14–0.19 (0.37 → 0.14, 0.41 → 0.15, 0.36 → 0.18, 0.42 → 0.19), around the closed form 1/6. AUC is unchanged because the map is monotone. *Framework: quantify.*

**J9. Show the leakage you are warning about.**

We ran the classic mistake on purpose and quarantined it: SMOTE on all 29,965 rows, then a split. The forest then shows "test" AUC 0.914 against the honest in-fold SMOTE 0.774, and even the logit 0.770 against 0.762 (`results/imbalance_leakage_demo.csv`). The inflation is mechanical: 72.6 % of the leaky test defaulters are synthetic points interpolated from training defaulters, so the model is asked to recognise its own neighbours — restricted to real rows the leaky forest's AUC falls to 0.845, and on synthetic-defaulters-vs-real-goods it reaches 0.940. The frozen manifest holds this run as a negative control so that nobody mistakes it for a result. *Framework: quantify.*

**J10. Your CV and test AUCs agree almost exactly for every model. Isn't that suspicious?**

It is what an honest protocol produces: for all eleven headline models the test AUC is within one CV standard deviation of the CV AUC (XGBoost 0.7863 → 0.7887, logistic 0.7602 → 0.7636, KNN 0.7700 → 0.7706, MLP 0.7762 → 0.7731), using the RBF SVM's full-training OOF 0.7606 rather than its subsample CV (`results/model_comparison.csv`). Suspicious would be the opposite — test far above CV — which is the signature of a leak, exactly what the quarantined SMOTE demonstration shows (0.914 vs 0.774). Training error is never used to compare models; train AUC 1.000 for the unpruned tree and 0.997 for the over-parameterised MLP are quoted only to show the optimism gap. The saved `results/*_p_oof.npy` and `*_p_test.npy` vectors let anyone recompute every number. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*

**J11. Why does the ranking slide order models by CV and not by test AUC?**

Because the test set may only report a model chosen without it — otherwise it becomes another validation set, which is the professor's 30 Sep warning. `code/aggregate.py` orders the eleven headline models by `select_auc` (training CV AUC; OOF for the RBF SVM) and picks the reference and the top four — XGBoost, GBM, random forest, MLP — on that column; the pruned tree is 6th on CV; its test AUC 0.7755 happens to exceed the MLP's and KNN's, which we report but do not rank by, and we present it that way (`results/model_comparison.csv`, `rank_cv_auc`; `results/aggregate_summary.json`, `selection`). The test columns sit beside the CV columns with bootstrap intervals, and the CV winner happens to be the test leader, which costs nothing to say but was not the criterion. *Framework: quantify.*

**J12. How do you know a difference between two models is real?**

Two scales. The fold-to-fold CV standard deviation (0.007–0.011 AUC) says whether a grid choice matters; the paired bootstrap of the same 8,990 test rows (B = 500, seed 2607526) says whether a test difference exceeds sampling noise (`results/auc_bootstrap_ci.csv`). Against XGBoost (bootstrap means, `auc_diff_mean`): forest +0.0020 [−0.0010, +0.0051] (a tie — XGBoost fails to beat it in 11 % of replicates), GBM +0.0015 [+0.0001, +0.0030] (borderline), then clearly below — pruned tree +0.0132 [+0.0082, +0.0184], MLP +0.0156 [+0.0103, +0.0199], KNN +0.0181 [+0.0125, +0.0237], logistic +0.0249 [+0.0183, +0.0314], SVM +0.0271, LDA +0.0293, naive Bayes +0.0486. Differences inside the linear family (≤ 0.005) are noise. *Framework: quantify.*

**J13. Why is the threshold more important than the model, and how big is each effect?**

At 5:1 the logistic regression at the default 0.5 costs 7,063 cost units on the 8,990 test clients (flag nobody 9,945, flag everybody 7,001); moving its cut-off to the training-OOF optimum 0.15 saves 1,937 [1,604, 2,218], 27 % of the cost, while switching to XGBoost at its own cut-off saves a further 145 [−18, 299] — an interval that includes zero (`results/threshold_vs_model_saving.csv`, paired bootstrap B = 1,000). The mechanism: a missed defaulter costs five false alarms, so the optimal rule flags anyone above probability 1/(1 + r) = 0.167, far below 0.5; at 0.5 every calibrated headline model catches only 32–37 % of defaulters (`recall_1_0_5` in `results/model_comparison.csv`; the exceptions are the over-confident naive Bayes, 58 %, and the RBF SVM, 7 %), at 0.15 the logit catches 74 % and XGBoost 78 % (`results/logistic.json`, `results/xgboost.json`, `at_tuned_cost`). No multiplier is used on the slide — two absolute savings with intervals. *Framework: quantify.*

**J14. Is the 5:1 conclusion robust to the cost ratio? What happens at 20:1?**

The threshold saving grows with the ratio — 238 [141, 338] at 2:1, 684 at 3:1, 1,937 at 5:1, 7,121 at 10:1, 20,335 at 20:1 — while the model saving stays between 87 and 450 and its interval includes zero at 5:1 and 20:1 (`results/threshold_vs_model_saving.csv`). At 20:1 the logistic cut-off drops to 0.01 and 99.9 % of clients are flagged, so the rule has degenerated into "flag everyone" (cost 6,993 against the 7,001 of flagging literally everyone); the threshold "saving" there is the trivial rule, not a model result, and we say so. The searched cut-offs track the closed form 1/(1 + r) at every ratio (0.33 vs 0.333 at 2:1, 0.10 vs 0.091 at 10:1), which is the sanity check that the OOF search is doing what theory says. *Framework: quantify.*

**J15. Why did you build the freeze machinery yourself instead of simply not touching the test set?**

Because by 18 Sep the test set had already scored 34 configurations across eight model families, and the professor's comment was precisely that further decisions would turn it into a validation set. A rule in people's heads is not auditable; a manifest is: `code/freeze.py` records each configuration's hash and its test-probability hash at first scoring, `common.evaluate()` refuses a changed configuration under a frozen slug before any label is read, and `--check` re-verifies all 46 entries (`results/frozen_manifest.json`). The two decisions made after the comment — adopting RUAN's nested-CV choices and the Platt variants — were taken on training rows and then scored once as new, registered configurations; the drop-column refits and TreeSHAP were moved to out-of-bag and training rows on 19 Oct. *Framework: acknowledge · reason · leave room.*
