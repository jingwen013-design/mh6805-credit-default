## Interpretation bullets (for the report)

- **What was tuned (course method).** `Pipeline(make_preprocessor(scale=False), GradientBoostingClassifier(subsample=0.8, random_state=2607526))`, grid n_estimators {200, 500} × learning_rate {0.03, 0.1} × max_depth {2, 3, 4} = 12 settings, 5-fold stratified CV (`common.CV`), criterion ROC AUC. One 500-tree sklearn GBM costs 35–65 s on the full training set, so the 60 grid fits (≈ 45 min) did not fit the 15-minute budget; two shortcuts were used and are recorded in the JSON `notes`: (a) the grid was scored on a stratified 50 % subsample of the *training* set (10,487 rows, seed 2607526), and (b) per (lr, depth) a single 500-tree model was fitted per fold and the AUC after 200 and after 500 trees was read from `staged_predict_proba` — exactly the 200- and 500-tree models, because boosting is sequential (the first 200 trees of a 500-tree model *are* the 200-tree model). The winner was refitted on the full training set and its CV AUC recomputed on the shared folds (0.7850 ± 0.0074; the subsample grid score was 0.7862 ± 0.0121). The grid took 301 s with two workers.
- **Best GBM: learning rate 0.03, depth 3, 200 trees (CV AUC 0.7862 on the subsample).** The ranking is instructive: every lr = 0.03 setting beats every lr = 0.1 setting except (0.1, depth 2, 200 trees), and the worst two settings are the *most* aggressive ones — lr 0.1 with depth 3/4 and 500 trees (CV AUC 0.7689 / 0.7635, training AUC 0.94 / 0.99). `figures/gbm_learning_curve.png` shows why: with lr 0.1 the CV curve peaks before 100 trees and then falls, i.e. later trees fit noise; with lr 0.03 it rises slowly to a flat top around 200–250 trees (staged optimum 230 trees, 0.7863) and drifts down only slightly by 500 (0.7850). Shrinkage is the regulariser here: small steps and shallow trees (3-way interactions at most) are what this noisy target rewards.
- **XGBoost (advanced method): full grid, no subsampling needed.** `XGBClassifier(objective='binary:logistic', eval_metric='auc', tree_method='hist', subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8, n_jobs=2, random_state=2607526)` inside the same pipeline; `GridSearchCV` over max_depth {3, 4, 6} × learning_rate {0.03, 0.1} × n_estimators {300, 600} × min_child_weight {1, 5} = 24 settings × 5 folds = 120 fits in 212 s (histogram splits make one 600-round fit 1–3 s, versus 35–65 s for sklearn's exact-split GBM). Best: depth 3, lr 0.03, 300 rounds, min_child_weight 5, CV AUC 0.7863 ± 0.0068 (the top four settings, 0.7859–0.7863, are all lr 0.03 / 300 rounds / depth 3–4, i.e. the same message as the sklearn grid). The worst setting is again the most flexible one, depth 6 / lr 0.1 / 600 rounds: CV AUC 0.7547 with a training AUC of 0.9984 — a textbook overfit. Refit on the full training set takes 0.5 s (vs 16.3 s for the sklearn GBM), which is the practical reason XGBoost is the industry default.
- **Learning curve (`figures/xgb_learning_curve.png`) — diminishing returns are visible in the number of rounds.** For the best depth/min_child_weight the 5-fold CV AUC with lr 0.03 is 0.7787 after 50 rounds, 0.7822 after 100, 0.7856 after 200, 0.7863 after 300, peaks at 0.7864 after 327 rounds and then declines (0.7850 at 600, 0.7827 at 1,000, 0.7794 at 1,500) while the training-fold AUC keeps climbing (0.8119 → 0.8665). With lr 0.1 the peak comes at 122 rounds (0.7858) and the decline is steeper (0.7611 at 1,500, training AUC 0.935). The best CV score with either learning rate is the same to three decimals, so the learning rate mostly trades off against the number of rounds; what matters is stopping near the peak, which is exactly what the grid's 300-round option does. Early stopping on a validation fold would have found the same point automatically.
- **Comparison with the logistic baseline: a clear but modest gain.** XGBoost test AUC 0.7887 vs 0.7637 (+0.025) and AP 0.5784 vs 0.5234 (+0.055); the sklearn GBM gives 0.7872 / 0.5759. The gain is 3–4 fold standard deviations (CV sd ≈ 0.007), so unlike the KNN edge it is real. Brier score improves from 0.1383 to 0.1311, so the boosted probabilities are also better calibrated. The source is non-linearity and interactions that a logit cannot express: PAY_1 is treated as a step function (paid duly vs 1 month late vs ≥ 2 months late), and the delay features interact with utilisation and limit.
- **Bagging vs boosting (random forest vs GBM/XGBoost): variance reduction vs bias reduction, same ceiling.** Random forest averages 500 deep, de-correlated trees grown independently on bootstrap samples — it reduces variance but each tree is a low-bias, high-variance learner (RF training AUC 0.8876 vs test 0.7866). Boosting fits shallow trees sequentially, each one to the residual (gradient) of the current ensemble, so it reduces bias with weak learners and the optimism gap is small (XGBoost training AUC 0.8082 vs test 0.7887). On this data set the two routes arrive at the same place: RF 0.7866 / 0.5756, GBM 0.7872 / 0.5759, XGBoost 0.7887 / 0.5784 (test AUC / AP), and the CV AUCs 0.7849, 0.7850, 0.7863 differ by less than one fold standard deviation (0.007). The honest statement for the report is "boosting is marginally ahead of the random forest, and the difference is within CV noise". Boosting is, however, far cheaper to store and score (300 depth-3 trees ≈ 4,500 nodes vs 500 unpruned trees with hundreds of leaves each), and the XGBoost fit is 40× faster than the forest.
- **Diminishing returns on this data.** Test AUC climbs logistic 0.764 → pruned tree 0.776 → bagging 0.783 → random forest 0.787 → GBM 0.787 → XGBoost 0.789 and then stops: none of the tree ensembles, however tuned, gets past ≈ 0.79, and pushing model capacity further (deeper trees, more rounds, higher learning rate) only *lowers* CV AUC. This looks like the Bayes-error ceiling of the problem rather than a tuning failure — six months of repayment history, a 22 % base rate and no income/bureau data leave most of the outcome unpredictable — so the marginal value of a more powerful learner is now small, and the choice among RF / GBM / XGBoost should be made on cost, interpretability and operational grounds rather than on the third decimal of AUC.
- **Class weighting changes the operating point, not the ranking.** With `scale_pos_weight = n_neg/n_pos = 3.5195` (best unweighted hyper-parameters kept), test AUC (0.7887 vs 0.7887) and AP (0.5788 vs 0.5784) are unchanged and CV AUC is 0.7861 vs 0.7863 — weighting re-scales the gradient of the positive class, which shifts scores upward almost monotonically. What changes is calibration: the mean out-of-fold probability rises from 0.221 (= the base rate) to 0.424, Brier worsens from 0.1311 to 0.1772 and log-loss from 0.4205 to 0.5359, and at the default 0.5 cut-off recall jumps from 0.363 to 0.649 (precision 0.702 → 0.461, accuracy 0.825 → 0.755). Tuning the threshold on the *unweighted* model achieves the same thing without breaking the probabilities (F1-optimal threshold 0.27: recall 0.5943, precision 0.5115, F1 0.5498 vs 0.5495 for the weighted model at its own threshold 0.55). For a lender who needs a probability of default (pricing, provisioning), the unweighted model plus an explicit threshold is the right deliverable.
- **Operating points and what they cost.** At threshold 0.5 XGBoost is accurate (0.8249 vs majority-class 0.779) but catches only 36 % of defaulters (722 of 1,989; 307 false alarms). At the F1-optimal threshold 0.27 (chosen on training out-of-fold probabilities) it catches 59 % (1,182) at precision 0.51 (1,129 false alarms); at the 5:1 cost-optimal threshold 0.15 it catches 78 % (1,559) at precision 0.36, test cost 4,981 vs 6,642 at 0.5 and 5,141 for logistic at its own cost threshold. The GBM numbers are within a few clients of these (F1 threshold 0.26: recall 0.5918, precision 0.5111; cost threshold 0.14: recall 0.8024, cost 5,060).
- **Feature importance (`figures/xgb_importance.png`, gain = total loss reduction, one-hot dummies summed).** Three repayment-delay features carry 68 % of the total gain: PAY_1 0.329, MAX_DELAY 0.212, N_MONTHS_DELAYED 0.144 — while using only 6.6 %, 2.2 % and 3.9 % of the splits (gain per split 173 / 335 / 129 vs ≈ 13–19 for the amount features). The balance and limit features (UTIL_MEAN 0.042, BILL_AMT1 0.031, UTIL_1 0.030, LIMIT_BAL 0.029) are split on most often (each ≈ 8 % of splits) but each split buys little: they refine the score within a delay stratum. SEX/MARRIAGE do not enter the top 15 and EDUCATION has 0.9 % of the gain, which is reassuring from a fairness standpoint. The ranking agrees with the random-forest MDI ranking (Spearman 0.61 over 29 features; the same top three) and with the logistic coefficients: the engineered MAX_DELAY / N_MONTHS_DELAYED features were worth building.
- **Caveats.** (i) The sklearn grid was scored on a 50 % subsample, so the subsample CV AUCs (sd ≈ 0.012) are noisier than the full-data XGBoost grid (sd ≈ 0.007); the *choice* of setting is robust (the same lr 0.03 / depth 3 corner wins in both grids) but the refitted model's CV AUC (0.7850) is the number to quote. (ii) Only two learning rates and two tree counts were tried per method; the learning curve shows the optimum lies between the grid points (≈ 230 trees for GBM, ≈ 330 rounds for XGBoost), so a finer search or early stopping could add ≈ 0.0005 AUC at most. (iii) The class-weighted variant reuses the unweighted hyper-parameters rather than being re-tuned. (iv) Boosting differences of 0.001–0.002 AUC between the three ensembles are below the fold-to-fold noise; a DeLong test on the saved `results/*_p_test.npy` vectors would be needed to claim any ordering. (v) `fit_seconds` are wall-clock times on a laptop shared with four other model scripts (load ≈ 20) and are only indicative; metrics are fully seeded and reproducible (`random_state=2607526`, xgboost 3.4.1, sklearn 1.9.0).
