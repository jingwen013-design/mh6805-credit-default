\section{Choosing the operating point}

\paragraph{Why 0.5 is the wrong cut-off, and 5:1 a scenario, not a fact.}
A cut-off of 0.5 is optimal only when both errors cost the same. With 22\% defaulters few clients reach a PD of 0.5: on the test set the frozen logit at 0.5 catches only 32\% of defaulters and, at 5:1, costs more than flagging everyone (Table~\ref{tab:a-operating}). The 5:1 ratio is borrowed from the German credit data (Statlog cost matrix [11]), not measured for this issuer. Our rough cost anchors point higher. If a missed defaulter loses 50--70\% of the median defaulter's bill, and a false alarm forgoes one to three months of a 1.5\% monthly margin on the median good client's bill, the ratio is 9.6:1 to 40.5:1. That is an upper bound, as some defaulters later pay (Table~\ref{tab:a-costsens}). A bank should read its own ratio off the 1:1--20:1 sweep (Table~\ref{tab:saving}) and set the cut-off on training OOF scores.

\paragraph{The cut-off, not the ensemble, is the lever.}
Moving the cut-off saves far more than changing the model. At $r$:1 one caught defaulter is worth $r$ false alarms, so the cheapest cut-off is where only one in $r + 1$ of the next clients flagged would default. For a calibrated model that is a PD of $1/(1+r)$, 0.167 at 5:1, close to the searched 0.15 for the logit and XGBoost (badly calibrated naive Bayes needs a higher one; training OOF; Figures~\ref{fig:a-rocpr-oof}, \ref{fig:all-pr}). At 5:1 on the test set, moving the logit's cut-off saves 1,937 cost units (false-alarm equivalents); switching to XGBoost saves a further 145, but its interval includes zero. At 10:1, nearer our anchors, both savings are clearly positive (Table~\ref{tab:saving}). The 5:1 flag is broad: XGBoost flags about half the test clients, 1.8 false alarms per defaulter caught (Table~\ref{tab:a-operating}), so it suits only a cheap action such as a reminder.

\paragraph{The best ranker need not be the cheapest.}
AUC averages over all cut-offs, while a bank works at one, where ROC curves can cross: KNN, with a higher AUC than the logit, costs more at 5:1 on test (5,219 against 5,126; Table~\ref{tab:headline}). Among the tied ensembles the cheapest rotates as the ratio moves from 1:1 to 15:1 (training OOF; Table~\ref{tab:a-ratio-pref}), so we name no cost winner. Choose the model for ranking, the cut-off from the bank's costs, and calibrate; above about 15:1 most are flagged, so a model's value lies in ordering a graded action (reminders for most, calls for the riskiest).

\paragraph{A one-line rule, and who is on the first call list.}
``Flag anyone with any payment delay in six months'' has no fitted parameter yet gets most of the way to the models' cost; it misses the 35.5\% of defaulters never late, where balances, limit and utilisation earn their place. At 5:1 in training CV XGBoost costs 7.7\% less than the rule (5/5 folds), the logit almost nothing (post-freeze; Table~\ref{tab:a-simplerules}; the rule was the cheapest of four candidates on these rows, a mild selection effect). A team that can call only the riskiest 10\% gains little from a model. On training OOF scores, XGBoost's top 10\% catches 31.5\% of defaulters and September status alone 30.9\%, because the top of any ranking is filled with clients already two or more months late (Table~\ref{tab:a-topk}). A model's value over the one-line rule is real but modest and lies further down the list: size the action to capacity.
