\begin{abstract}
  Banks use default models as cut-offs, so we judge ranking and
  operating point. On the UCI credit-card data (29,965 cardholders, 22.1\%
  defaulters, drawn as the 2005 card-debt crisis began) we compare ten
  classifiers, choosing everything by training cross-validation (CV) and
  freezing each configuration at its first test scoring. XGBoost, gradient
  boosting and the random forest tie (CV AUC 0.785--0.786; XGBoost test 0.789),
  0.025--0.026 above the logit. Post-freeze training-CV checks show where
  the signal lies: adding September's record to limit and demographics lifts
  XGBoost's AUC from 0.63 to 0.77; a third of the logit's gap is reading
  repayment codes as evenly spaced numbers, most of the rest one-variable curves;
  interactions are small; demographics add 0.002 CV AUC but raise fairness
  issues. Resampling before the split fakes a 0.914 AUC. At 5:1, moving the
  logit's cut-off saves 1,937 test cost units; switching to XGBoost saves 145
  more [$-$18, 299].
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{A default model in a crisis vintage.}
Every probability of default (PD) here belongs to a crisis vintage. The data come from Taiwan's 2005--06 card-debt crisis [19]: the central bank dates the first repayment problems to September 2005, our last feature month [4]. Revolving balances peaked at NT\$495.3 bn in November 2005 and fell 48.8\% by December 2008 [9]. Our 22.1\% default rate is consistent with a stressed book or an undocumented, defaulter-heavy sample (\S7, iii). Errors cost unequally: a missed defaulter's balance is lost; a wrongly flagged good client risks a thin margin.

\paragraph{Why explanations matter.}
Lenders are now expected to explain a credit score, so we pair accuracy with Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), monotone constraints and a logistic scorecard. The EU AI Act classes creditworthiness scoring of individuals as high-risk (Annex~III, 5(b)) [8]. Singapore's FEAT principles ask that customers can learn which data were used and how they affected a decision (Principle~13) [15]; these are present-day benchmarks, and we claim no compliance. In the 2024 Bank of England--FCA survey, gradient boosting was the most common model type (32\% of use cases) and SHAP (64\%) among the most popular explainability methods [1].

\paragraph{Design and objectives.}
The study is comparative [12]: a $K$-nearest-neighbour (KNN) benchmark and a logistic scorecard are the references, each other family is explained by how it learns, and TreeSHAP [14], monotone XGBoost [6] and partial dependence [10] go beyond the syllabus. We ask (1)~\emph{complexity}: what flexible models add beyond noise; (2)~\emph{operating point}: whether the cut-off matters more than the model; (3)~\emph{imbalance}: what weighting and resampling do inside the folds and before the split; (4)~\emph{inference}: which characteristics predict default, in which direction, what personal attributes add at what fairness cost, and whether the importance measures agree.
